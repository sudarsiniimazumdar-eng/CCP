V. POCKET

A subtle, wearable quick-fix toolkit designed to restore immediate dignity, physical comfort, and autonomy when lower-limb prosthetics fail during transit in urban and rural India.

01

System Context & The Fragmented Repair Ecosystem

In urban and rural India, persons with disabilities (PWDs) using lower-limb prosthetics and orthotics operate within an infrastructure that is fundamentally fragile. While assistive technology is designed to enable continuous mobility, daily reality is marked by recurrent mechanical breakdowns, socket instability, socket loosening from perspiration, and an acute absence of immediate roadside or workplace maintenance support.

When a mechanical element fails—whether a suspension strap shears, an anchor pin loosens, or volume reduction in the residual limb causes painful socket slippage—the formal repair ecosystem offers no immediate recourse. Formal clinical centers and specialized fabrication workshops are concentrated in distant urban hubs. Accessing them demands extensive travel, significant out-of-pocket expenses, and lost work days.

Consequently, amputees are forced to rely on improvisational jugaad solutions: binding broken straps with packing tape, stuffing scrap fabrics or plastic bags into loose sockets, or wedging rubber scraps under their feet. These makeshift fixes are mechanically precarious, physically injurious, and socially humiliating. A minor mechanical failure abruptly strips an individual of their independence in the middle of a workday, market commute, or farm shift.

Systemic Problem Statement

The assistive device ecosystem in India suffers from a severe operational gap between daily physical wear and formal clinical repair. Users lack a portable, body-integrated first-aid mechanism to solve immediate mechanical and comfort failures on the spot.

02

Ecosystem Mapping & Multi-Persona Synthesis

To understand the touchpoints between users, formal institutions, and informal repair workarounds, an exhaustive ecosystem analysis was conducted alongside multi-stakeholder mapping and qualitative persona synthesis.

Mapping 6 Diverse User Realities

Because lower-limb prosthetic users in India encompass vastly different economic, physical, and occupational realities, AI-assisted qualitative synthesis was utilized to construct six distinct user personas. Rather than designing for a generic "average amputee," the inquiry stress-tested constraints across contrasting daily routines:

  • Agricultural Farmer: Navigating muddy, uneven terrain under extreme heat; high risk of sweat-induced socket slippage and dust intrusion.
  • Small Business Owner / Shopkeeper: Standing for 10+ hours in cramped stalls; repetitive weight transfer and lack of sitting breaks.
  • College Student: Frequent public transit commutes, stairs, and intense social anxiety surrounding visible device slippage or audible strap failure.
  • Last-Mile Delivery Worker: High physical exertion, motorcycle vibrations, and zero time to visit a distant workshop during delivery shifts.
  • Primary School Teacher: Continuous standing and walking across classrooms; requires discreet, instant adjustments without leaving students unattended.
  • Homemaker: Navigating wet kitchen floors, continuous domestic chores, and handling heavy objects with compromised balance.

Observed Failure Modes

Across all six personas, the qualitative research synthesized four recurrent, high-stress friction points:

  1. Socket Loosening & Stump Volume Fluctuation: As users walk and perspire throughout the day, the residual limb loses fluid volume, causing the socket to loosen, rattle, and abrade against the skin.
  2. Suspension Strap Shearing: Mechanical fatigue on Velcro, buckles, or fabric bindings leads to sudden tearing while in motion.
  3. Object Grip & Bilateral Dexterity Gaps: Amputees carrying bags or opening transit gates frequently lack stable secondary grip or leverage.
  4. Underfoot Instability: Worn prosthetic foot bumpers or muddy surfaces cause sudden traction loss on unpaved roads.
03

Ideation & Divergent Systems Exploration

Ideation commenced with wide divergent brainstorming, generating over 30 initial concepts through personal analogy, morphological clustering, and systematic constraint comparison. These concepts were synthesized into four competing systemic directions:

System Architecture Core Mechanism Key Strengths Critical Shortcoming
1. Mobility Assist Underfoot auxiliary gripper, drag-assist footplate, traction bands Directly improves mechanical grip on wet or unpaved terrain Did not address socket comfort, loosening, or strap breakage
2. Field Repair Toolkit Keychain or wallet multi-tool with Allen keys, spare pins, clips Comprehensive mechanical repair capacity for complex fittings Heavy, intimidating, bulky, and difficult to operate during transit
3. Integrated Carry Sleeve Shin sleeve with modular storage pockets for everyday items Discreetly hides equipment against the prosthetic pylon Exacerbated heat and sweating around the limb; hard to reach
4. Adaptive Comfort System Air-pocket cushion, modular emergency strap, silicone grip ring Lightweight, fast to deploy, directly addresses dignity and fit Requires compact form factor to avoid bumping against garments

From these four architectures, two primary prototypes advanced into detailed CAD exploration: Core Idea 1 (Keychain/Wallet Multi-Tool) and Core Idea 2 (Multipurpose Leg Strap System).

04

The Honest Pivot: Discarding the Multi-Tool

The initial inclination was to engineer a comprehensive "mechanical first aid kit"—a rugged multi-tool equipped with tightening bits, hex wrenches, replacement clamps, and heavy fasteners. However, critical evaluation of user contexts quickly revealed a fundamental flaw in this approach.

Critical Failure & Design Pivot

Why the Multi-Tool was Discarded: Field tools require fine manual dexterity, clean working surfaces, and mechanical knowledge. When an amputee suffers a breakdown on an overcrowded bus or in a rainstorm, they cannot disassemble hardware. Complex tools added excessive weight, rattled audibly, and placed a high cognitive burden on an already distressed user.

The project underwent a decisive strategic pivot: Shift from mechanical repair to adaptive stabilization. The objective was no longer to replace a workshop, but to provide a 60-second intervention that stabilizes the user until they reach safety.

"A product is supposed to be so simple it goes unnoticed in daily life; instead of trying to solve multiple systemic issues, focus on aiding just 2–3 immediate physical pain points." — Project Learning Reflection
05

Final Concept & Component Specification

The resulting intervention is V. POCKET: an ultra-compact, weather-resistant physical add-on pouch that integrates directly onto the leg strap, belt, or wrist. Secured via a self-aligning magnetic latch and breathable Velcro backing, V. POCKET houses exactly three targeted physical components engineered for immediate crisis mitigation:

Component Module Material Specification Mechanical Function Crisis Touchpoint
1. Inflatable Air Pocket Medical-grade TPU bladder, micro hand-bulb valve Inserted into the prosthetic socket; rapidly fills volume voids caused by limb sweating and muscle atrophy. Socket loosening, internal bruising, instability during walking.
2. Expandable Grip Ring Textured elastomeric silicone / ribbed rubber Stretches over bottles, door knobs, or bus rails to provide high friction leverage with minimal grip force. Weak hand grip, transit stabilization, opening sealed containers.
3. Emergency Tension Strap High-tensile nylon webbing, heavy-duty cam buckle Wraps externally around broken socket suspension or failing calf braces to restore structural tension. Sudden strap shearing, hinge failure, temporary walk-home fix.

2-in-1 Conversion & Non-Intrusive Onboarding

V. POCKET is designed with dual-mode adaptability. While its primary form attaches securely around the prosthetic pylon or upper thigh via a padded strap, it can also detach instantly to convert into a compact keychain or wristband pouch. This flexibility allows users wearing traditional Indian attire (such as sarees, dhotis, or lungis) to carry the unit without awkward garment interference.

06

Physical Interaction Design & User Storyboard

To validate the temporal flow of emergency intervention, a 4-stage narrative storyboard was constructed, tracking a user through an acute crisis scenario during an everyday commute:

  1. Stage 1 (Everyday Transit): The user is commuting on a crowded commuter train in humid conditions. V. POCKET sits flush against their leg under trousers without bulk or noise.
  2. Stage 2 (Failure Moment): Heavy foot traffic forces a sudden pivot; excess sweat causes the prosthetic socket to slip downward, threatening a catastrophic fall.
  3. Stage 3 (Subtle Intervention): The user unclasps V. POCKET's magnetic flap with one hand, extracts the Air Pocket, slips it between the limb and inner socket, and gives two quick pumps.
  4. Stage 4 (Restored Autonomy): Immediate volume stability is restored in under 45 seconds. The user resumes walking steadily without needing to ask strangers for assistance or sit on the station floor.

08

Retrospective & Product Management Signals

V. POCKET demonstrates that effective product thinking often lies in what a designer chooses not to build. By actively rejecting the allure of a feature-heavy multi-tool, the project converged on an intervention that honors the user's emotional dignity, bodily comfort, and temporal urgency.

  • Systems-Level Problem Framing: Successfully mapped a massive, unaddressed institutional void in India's disability infrastructure.
  • Ruthless Feature Prioritization: Reduced 30+ ideation concepts to exactly 3 focused, high-leverage physical interventions.
  • Physical UX & Dignity: Designed for emotional invisibility—ensuring the intervention creates no stigmatizing attention during public failure.
